# Dice and Rolls
Each player starts the game with six (6) unique dice to assign to different stats (see below). 

- **D20 - superb**: anyone meeting you in passing can immediately tell you're good at this.
- **D12 - impressive**: people who know you would say you're good at this thing, but it may not be obvious at first glance.
- **D10 - above average**: you're not great, but you are good.
- **D8 - below average**: you're not bad, but you aren't decent either.
- **D6 - bad**: people who know you would say you're bad at this thing, but it may not be obvious at first glance.
- **D4 - terrible**: anyone meeting you in passing can immediately tell you're bad at this.

**Exploding dice** - if you roll the maximum value of your die, your die "explodes," and you roll again. Repeat until you do NOT hit the maximum value on your die. Add up all the values that you rolled. Once you succeed at a roll, stop rolling (e.g., DC 14 and you rolled a 20? You succeeded, so stop). Narratively, an "exploded" success should come in part from forces outside of the character (e.g., a lucky break, or an assist from an NPC).

**Advantage and Disadvantage** - with advantage, roll your entire dice pool twice, resolving each pool's explosions fully, and take the higher of the two totals. With disadvantage, roll twice the same way and take the lower total.

**Planned Actions vs Snap Decisions**: Planned Actions are checks that occur without time pressure and under ideal circumstances. You can automatically succeed a Planned Action whose difficulty is half your die size or smaller in that skill (e.g., DC 10 on a Brains check with a D20 in Brains) without rolling. Snap decisions are the opposite: time pressure and nonideal circumstances. You roll straight, and your party cannot spend Determination Tokens to help your roll.

# Determination Tokens

Every time you fail a check, take a Determination Token. All characters can expend a token to increase a roll by +1 per token used. For Planned Actions, other party members can expend tokens on other people's rolls. Determination Tokens cannot be spent to assist another character's roll in combat, since combat actions are Snap Decisions by default — unless a specific ability says otherwise. A failed attack in combat still grants a Determination Token, same as any other failed check. A token may be spent **after the roll is seen** (table ruling, Session One), and on a Planned Action tokens may be pooled in the middle of the table and donated.

## Degrees of Success

Succeeding a check by 10 or more is an unbelievable success. Treat it like rolling a critical.

Succeeding by 5 or more should be flavored to make it look effortless for the character.

Failure by 5 or more should have nonserious short term side effects.

Failure by 10 or more should have serious short term side effects, and potentially long lasting ones.

# Alignment

Alignment is political before it is moral. A character is aligned to a **faction**, not to an abstract good or evil. See [Factions](../Lore/Factions.md).

## The Scale

| Tier           | DC change | Also                |
| -------------- | --------- | ------------------- |
| **Enemy**      | +20%      | Roll at disadvantage |
| **Misaligned** | +20%      | —                   |
| **Neutral**    | none      | —                   |
| **Aligned**    | -20%      | —                   |
| **Ally**       | -20%      | Roll at advantage   |

Alignment changes the difficulty of the task, not the character's skill. A stranger is not worse at talking. The room is worse at listening.

**Alignment always modifies the reactive side.** For a check against a DC, adjust the DC. For an opposed check, adjust the reactive character's rolled total. The proactive character's own roll is never touched.

**Rounding.** Round the adjusted DC to the nearest whole number. The change is never less than 1. Any result below 1 is a DC of 1, which succeeds automatically.

**Order of operations.** Adjust the DC first. Then check the Planned Action auto-success threshold against the adjusted DC. A Misaligned character loses auto-successes they would otherwise get.

The percentage scales with the task. At DC 20 the shift is 4. At DC 5 it is 1. Hard tasks punish bad standing more than easy ones.

Alignment applies to:

- Social checks with members of that faction.
- Gifted magic, in both casting and resistance. [Gifted Magic](../Lore/Magic%20System.md#gifted-magic) states that Gifted magic is the source most strongly affected by alignment.

**Alignment does not apply to combat.**

Gifted magic is the single exception. It applies in combat as it does everywhere else. Each Gifted spell states this in its own body. See [Spells](../Mechanics/Abilities.md#spells).

**The exception keys on the target.** Alignment enters only for the characters a spell targets. A Gifted spell cast on allies is unaffected by the alignment of enemies in the scene — they are not the target, so their standing is irrelevant to it. A Gifted spell that targets enemies brings alignment into play in full, for casting and for resistance. A spell that does both does each on its own targets.

## Default Alignment

**Every character starts Misaligned with every faction.** A faction the character has never met, and has no history with, treats that character as a stranger. Strangers are suspect.

This is deliberate. The default is not Neutral. Neutral is earned.

## Racial Identity

**Shared racial identity with a faction raises alignment by one tier.** Apply this before any other modifier.

A Drau meeting the Drau of the City starts at Neutral, not Misaligned.

This is a starting position, not a fixed one. A character can be an Enemy of a faction of their own race.

## Shifting Alignment

Plot points move alignment. A faction's [Current Move](../Lore/Factions.md) is the usual trigger. The GM shifts a character one tier at a time, in either direction, when the story earns it.

## Regional Familiarity

Knowledge keyed to a place runs on the same track, from **unfamiliar to familiar**. A character keys off the region they are from — "the UKs" for the current party — and gains familiarity with a new region by spending time in it. It gates what a Wisdom or knowledge roll can turn up: a stranger to a city knows nothing a local would; a character who has spent real time there knows what an ordinary street person knows, and more through their own faction and social ties under the normal alignment rules. After Sessions Zero–Two all four PCs are at least **Neutrally familiar with New Squishnesta**.

Abilities are modifiers that give nuance to your character's stat block. For instance, "Prepared" may allow the user to to use proactive skills for defensive checks, whereas "Improviser" may allow the user to use reactive skills for proactive skill checks. These abilities may be permament (true growth) or temporary (to suggest a condition).

Your character may start with a set of these abilities and acquire new ones through story beats. Character flaws and strengths SHOULD be captured as abilities.

# Initiative

At the start of a combat or conflict, every character rolls Prowess (exploding) to determine turn order. Highest total acts first. Some abilities — see [Quick](../Mechanics/Abilities.md#quick) — allow a character to roll Instinct instead.

You only roll initiative once per combat. That order holds for the entire fight, even across multiple rounds.

**Ties** are broken by comparing Prowess die size (a d12 beats a d8, regardless of the roll). If the dice are the same size, the GM decides.

All actions taken during a combat are Snap Decisions by default. An ability may state otherwise.

## Surprise

A group can only surprise another group while stealthed (see **Stealth**, below). If the attacking group is stealthed when it takes its first action, it triggers a surprise round.

**The free round.** The surprising group gets one full round — movement, action, and object interaction — before the surprised group does anything at all.

**The Surprised condition.** Each member of the surprised group rolls Instinct. Anyone who fails is Surprised. Being Surprised has no bearing on whether the free round happens — that's automatic — it only means attacks against that character get advantage, and some abilities may grant that character's own attacks or defenses advantage or disadvantage in kind.

Surprised lasts one turn. It can be extended past that only if the entire surprising group stays stealthed through the end of their turn — meaning nobody in the group has attacked yet. The moment any attack lands, the attacker (and only the attacker) drops out of stealth.

# Stealth

Stealth is a Prowess roll — you're proactively making yourself hard to notice.

The GM sets the DC using the party's average Instinct die size, or may instead call for a straight opposed roll: your Prowess against the target's Instinct.

Terrain can shift the DC at the GM's discretion. Forests, for instance, improve stealth DC, and also impose disadvantage on ranged attacks made into them.

Once in combat, re-entering stealth costs a full action. Movement and object interaction are unaffected and can still happen on that turn.

Stealth breaks the instant an attack lands on a target — not when the attack is declared, and not on a miss.

# Combat

All combat actions are Snap Decisions, and many are opposed checks. In the case of Physical Damage, the attacker rolls Prowess against the defender's Constitution (or appropriate stat). Projectile Damage uses the attacker's Prowess or Intelligence (at GM's discression). All combat scenarios risk character injury or death, and there are no "pulled punches," "called shots," or "incapacitating blows". 

| **Attacker - Defender** | Narrative Result                                                                                                                                                                                                               | Health-Track Clicks |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- |
| +0 or lower             | Defender is uninjured and narrates outcome.                                                                                                                                                                                    | 0 |
| +1 to +3                | Attacker narrates the attack and defender narrates the defense. Defender is hurt superficially and temporarily (e.g., stunned from a punch).                                                                                   | 1 |
| +4 to +6                | Attacker narrates the attack, defender narrates the defense, and attacker explains how they overcome the defense. Defender is hurt moderately (e.g., concussed, cracked rib)                                                   | 2 |
| +7 to +9                | Attacker narrates the attack and can alter details of the defender's narrative of the defense. The attacker then explains how the defense is overcome. Defender is badly hurt (e.g., unconscious, loosing blood, broken bones) | 3 |
| +10 or more             | The defender is dead or nearly. Immediate medical attention may save their life, but they will have permanent health issues (e.g., bullet between the eyes, stabbed through the chest).                                        | 4 |

**One system.** The margin table and the Health track are one system, not two: each band above steps the Health track (see below) down by the listed number of clicks. A defender starting at Normal who takes a +10-or-more hit drops the full four clicks straight to Incapacitated, matching the table's own "dead or nearly" language. See also Rules Resolution - R-1 Margin vs Health.

A spell or weapon may deal multiple steps of damage at once, above and beyond what the margin table implies, if its own description says so — there's no upper limit.

**Unarmed attacks.** A character with no weapon rolls Prowess at disadvantage by default — bare-handed combat is worse than fighting armed. This is waived only for a character who is narratively expected to fight well unarmed (a trained brawler, a martial artist), via the [Unarmed Strikes](../Mechanics/Abilities.md#unarmed-strikes) ability, which removes the disadvantage.

## Actions in Combat

On your turn you get one move, one action, and one object interaction.

**Reactions.** A character with an ability that can be used as a reaction gets **one reaction per turn**. Without such an ability there is no reaction to take. **Defending as a reaction likewise needs an ability**; otherwise it costs a full action, as below.

**Defending** is an action. You give up your attack this turn, and in exchange you get advantage on your next resistance roll against an incoming attack — through the end of the round.

## Zones and Movement

Combat uses **zones** instead of measured distance. A zone is roughly 30 feet across. Three zones, in a line, is the default setup for most encounters — this is a default, not a hard cap; a bar fight might only need two, a siege might need five.

A fourth "extended" zone exists conceptually for anything too far away to be part of the fight — nothing can be shot at or shot from extended distance. A character entering combat from extended distance does so narratively, by closing to Zone 3 first; depending on the fiction, this might take more than one turn.

A character moves one zone per turn by default, as their move for the turn. Some abilities change this.

**Vertical distance** adds into the total using rough geometric distance: one zone across and one zone up counts as two zones of distance overall.

**Flying and swimming** move in three dimensions; walking moves in two. This means a grounded character can't simply walk over to reach someone directly above them — they need an ability, or some object/interaction that gets them airborne, to close that gap in melee. A ranged attacker, on the other hand, can shoot a flying target normally, applying the usual range penalties for distance.

**Leaving a zone in melee.** You can't leave a zone that an enemy occupies with you without triggering an attack of opportunity, unless you first make a successful Prowess check to disengage. Success removes the attack of opportunity entirely; failure means you still leave, but the enemy gets their swing.

Attacks of opportunity can be made by any character with an enemy in their melee range. Some abilities extend this further, letting a character punish someone leaving an *ally's* melee range too.

**Movement and damage.** A character suffering reduced mobility from injury (see Health, below) must roll Constitution against a DC to cross a zone at all. The DC rises with the severity of the damage they're carrying. This is pushing through pain, not agility, so it draws on the same stat that already resists damage and affects.

## Ranged Combat

Weapons and spells have a stated range in zones — two zones is the default, though some weapons (as noted in their description) reach three.

Shooting one zone beyond a weapon's stated range imposes disadvantage. Beyond that, it's an automatic miss.

**Vaporised materials.** A substance delivered as vapour from a rig has a range of **two zones**; otherwise it uses the rolling mechanics of the material itself. Loading is preparation before combat, or an action during it.

You can shoot into a zone where an ally is fighting in melee, but you must make an additional Prowess check (GM sets the DC) to avoid hitting them. Fail that roll, and your ally takes the attack as if they were the intended target — full dice, full damage.

**Health** - typical players will progress across the following stages upon receiving damage (or healing) by one step (unless stated otherwise in the attack or healing action):

- **Buffed** - varies by buff, but a temporary status of extra health or strength.
- **Normal** - what it sounds like
- **Minor Damage** - cosmetic, glancing damage (e.g., a cut to the face). Does nothing to mechanically hinder your character.
- **Major Damage** - yields short term affects that remain until a long rest (e.g., cracked rib). Characters have reduced mobility OR must roll disadvantage in one stat (at GM's discression, based on type of damage sustained).
- **Critical Damage** - yields long term affects that remain until medically treated (e.g., broken leg). Characters have reduced mobility AND must roll disadvantage in proactive rolls.
- **Incapacitated** - character is unconscious and requires immediate medical attention. At risk of death.
- **Dead** - character is dead. A divine magic is required to revivify them.

**Incapacitated characters** do not roll saving throws. They can still roll Constitution. A finishing blow (a "coup de grace") is not a separate mechanic — it's the same margin-graded attack roll as any other: an enemy swinging on an incapacitated character rolls Prowess against their Constitution (or Intelligence against Wisdom, for a spell), and the margin steps the Health track down exactly per the table above. Because Incapacitated sits one click above Dead, even the lowest scoring band (+1 to +3, one click) is enough to kill outright. Only a total miss (+0 or lower, zero clicks) leaves them incapacitated. This is an attempt, not an automatic outcome, since the attack roll can still miss.

# Karmic Damage

A second track, beside Health, for a character whose karma is being spent or eaten. Most characters never touch it: their karma is **Normal** and stays there. A character with a karmic condition — so far only [Alistrassa](../Player%20Sheets/Alistrassa.md)'s wasting disease — tracks it on their sheet.

**Normal → Minor karmic damage → Major karmic damage → Critical karmic damage → Incorporeal → Extinct.**

- **Taking it.** When a karmic-wasting caster casts innately, she rolls **Constitution against DC 4** or takes physical damage on the Health track: **one point for a cantrip, two if a spell slot is spent, three for two slots.** When she casts to hurry a task she could have done slowly — reading a library in a night instead of two weeks — she takes **one point of karmic damage per action** instead of the time.
- **Extinct** is the end: no Celestial, no return.
- **Incorporeal** is the step before it. The body fades into an immaterial ether that **can be collected and held**. With karmic healing the person can be made corporeal again; without it they go on dissipating.
- **Healing it** has no method yet. Karmic restoration is the practice that would, and nobody alive has it — that is [Alistrassa's](../Player%20Sheets/Alistrassa.md) quest. Short of it only a divine act would serve: communing with something that outranks the Order. No mechanism exists.
- **The clock.** A wasting character also progresses without casting, as months and years pass. The GM advances it, roughly once a session when it fits.

Karmic damage is a cosmology mechanic, not a status condition: it does not impose disadvantage. What it changes is how much of you comes back.

# Casting

A spell is an action, unless the spell itself specifies otherwise. **Ritual** spells, and any spell requiring material components, must be cast as Planned Actions rather than Snap Decisions.

**Casting check.** The caster rolls their source's proactive stat — Prowess for Innate, Intelligence for Derived, Charisma for Gifted — against the target's resistance stat for that same source: Constitution resists Innate, Wisdom resists Derived, Instinct resists Gifted. Individual spells may call for a different stat instead.

Any source can attempt any school of magic — a Sorcerer can try their hand at alchemy, a Wizard at divine channeling — but a caster working outside their natural alignment (see the School affinity table under [Magic System](../Lore/Magic%20System.md)) will find it harder, purely by narrative and by their stat spread, not by any additional mechanical penalty layered on top.

**Cantrips and slots.** Entry-level spells are cantrips and cost nothing to cast. Beyond that, casting draws on spell slots. All slots refresh on a long rest; one slot refreshes on a short rest. Slots are granted like abilities — through story beats, at the GM's discretion, not on a fixed schedule.

**Upcasting.** Spending a higher slot on a spell either adds dice to the casting check's pool (if the spell specifies this) or increases the stated effect — more damage, longer duration — without touching the pool. Each spell states which applies.

**Damage.** Spell damage uses the same margin-based Health track as weapon damage (see Combat, above).

**Components.** Every spell's template includes a Components field: Material, Somatic, Verbal, or some combination. Material components generally belong to Derived magic, though some Gifted spells call for them too, and they force a Planned Action.

**Who can cast.** Casting is gated by ability, not by race. A character can cast if and only if they hold the relevant casting ability (see [Spells](../Mechanics/Abilities.md#spells)).

**Divination is played as a conversation with the GM.** A divination cast — an Identify that points you to the knowledge you need, a comprehension of a text — is an in-universe roll, not a meta roll, but it resolves as the player interrogating the GM about what the character could learn, within reason and paying the narrative cost. "It's funny because you were just saying you'd prefer people to interrogate you."

# The Moon Dice

The Moon is a Majestic Celestial and her attention has a shape — see [The Moon and the Zodiac](../Lore/Magic%20System.md#the-moon-and-the-zodiac). In play, two dice describe her.

**Once per night**, at the first moment the moon matters, roll both and keep the results until the next in-world night:

- **d12 — the sign.** Where the moon is in the zodiac. The time of year does not change inside a session, so one roll covers it; a session that spans several nights rolls again each night. Rolling stays even once a calendar exists.
- **d4 — the phase.** How strong the moon is, and which column of the sign table applies. The phase governs **every character aligned with the Moon** — [Iter](../Player%20Sheets/Iter%20Bravus.md) today; Bob if he ever formally aligns.

# Advancement

This is a milestone-driven game — characters accumulate abilities and equipment far more than they change their base dice. Every character gets story beats at roughly the same rate; whether a beat actually moves the needle depends on whether the character leans into it, and whether they succeed.

When a story beat calls for it, a character may:

- **Swap** two of their existing dice with each other, or
- **Upgrade or downgrade** a single die by one step (e.g., d8 to d10).

The GM decides which applies, based on what the beat was about.

The six-die permutation every character starts with (one each of d20 through d4) is a chargen rule, not a lifetime one. Over a campaign, a character's dice are expected to drift from that starting spread.

Spell slots and new abilities (including new spells) advance on this same story-beat trigger, at the same pace as dice.
