# Session Six — the bang, and the library

*Played 2026-10-07. Previously: Session Five, the party and the sunbeam.*

Snarfa's party was dying the way good parties do, slowly and with
casualties. Transmutation had poured sand into Ra's hole and turned it
to concrete. Snarfa had taken three points of something and gone to
sleep it off. Bob's other half still stood in the quad, legs and bare
skeleton, and Bob had decided he was not going to do that again. Ada
Crane had been gone about twenty minutes, to her office, for her books.
The party started up the stairs to Abjuration to see how Penny was
getting on.

They felt it before they heard it: a shudder through the stone, then a
sonic boom. Out of the windows a column of smoke went up fifty feet, on
the most direct line between the College and the Castle. That was the
road Ada had taken. Alistrassa's nose bled a little, for no reason
anyone could find. Iter went for her parasol and Alistrassa for her
cloak, on the principle that they were united in not dying again, and
then everybody went to the blast.

Nobody saw what happened there first. Ada lay under half a snapped beam,
slightly hurt, her ledgers spilled across the rubble. A small figure
came out of the soot, a girl in her early teens, with a long brown braid
and very baggy forest-green fatigues, hard to keep in view. She had
been watching Ada for some time. She gathered up the books and went
into the alleys.

On the College steps, at that moment, a hooded figure crouched at the
boom and checked that nobody had seen her get scared. Lark was back. She
was covered from head to foot now: a cloak over her head, a leather top,
long sleeves, gloves, trousers, her usual boots, her squirrels round her
neck and a rucksack in one arm, and not a gas tank in sight. Out of the
corner of her eye she saw a small figure darting toward the smoke, and
filed away which way it went. She knocked on the door of Abjuration at
the same moment the others reached it. The squirrel she had left with
the party had been asleep all this time in Snarfa's empty wine goblet.
It fell out, took the fall badly, and climbed onto her neck with the
others. He was her charm now. They were a chain.

Penny was at her studies. She had the canvases for Iter's parasol and
Alistrassa's cloak ready, the runes drawn by hand, still and not moving
the way her doodles usually do, and relatively powerful for all that.
Each of them could choose what the runes would hold off. Both chose
physical harm. Penny seemed a little distant, a little less warm than
she had been. It was the midterms, she said. Metamagic was a weed-out
course, and she was struggling with Divination. Alistrassa, who had
technically done some Divination back in the day, offered office hours,
and Penny brightened. Iter introduced Lark to Alistrassa. Lark asked
about her satchel. Alistrassa said the sand inside was somehow tied up
with karma; Bob kept coming back covered in it.

Outside, Evocation wizards were pouring water from their hands onto the
burning townhouses. Alistrassa read the scorch marks: a device, planted
at a choke point on the direct road, in a narrow alley that no longer
existed. Someone had meant to stop someone on the way from the College
to the Castle. Pudge lifted the beam off Ada. She had heard a loud
noise and dropped her papers, and the girl had got away with them:
five foot seven, dark hair in a fat ponytail, coveralls, heading for the
Red Line. Ada wanted to give chase. Alistrassa asked Pudge to walk
twenty feet ahead.

He found the next one with his feet. A stone gave on a small bridge
over the Brightwater, and Pudge went ten feet down into the river with
Seamus held clear, and both of them were swept downstream. Iter threw a
mushroom out across the water. Pudge caught it with one hand and
Seamus's rope with the other, and Seamus, who cannot scream, did not.
They climbed out on the near side. The bridge had a fifteen-foot gap in
it now, with the same scorch marks as the alley. Two explosions in one
night, Pudge concluded, pointed toward somebody who knew what they were
doing.

Bob, settled back into his body by the blast, saw her: a moonbeam fell
into an alley a hundred yards off and lit a runner turning a corner at a
full sprint. Not a Munchkin. Then the shadow at the edge of the light
reached out and pulled her out of it, and the moonlight could not find
her again. Iter grew a slimy, very bouncy mushroom in the gap of the
bridge and bounced across, and beckoned, and everybody followed. Lark
jumped.

It was a chase, and for a while the thief was winning it. Then the
party began to close, and she turned. You could see the fear in her
eyes, and something under it that had decided it could not fail. The
shadows around her took her shape, a larger and much worse version with
a hammer, and it screamed. Everyone reeled except Pudge, whose armour
kept it off, and he closed and tried to grab her, with Seamus in his
other hand, and something that was not quite there moved her out of his
way. He gave her an awkward grin. "We mean no harm." "If you knew
better," she said, "you would turn back now," and walked out of his
reach as if he were not there, and ran.

Pudge caught her again and could not keep her. Lark, sitting on the
ground at the back, shouted reasons. Bob, running, tried a thing he had
seen done: he asked his moth to help, nicely, and the Moon answered.
The moth lit up silver. Bob could see through its eyes. It was called
**Mothra** now, and it went through a small crack in time and space and
came out in the girl's face. Iter tripped over nothing and face-planted
on the cobbles. "God damn it, lady, we're just trying to help."

The girl pulled the nails out of a loose board as she passed a doorway,
and the lintel came down behind her. She went up a street tree toward
the rooftops. Lark introduced her squirrels to the table: Larry, Curly,
The Bald One I Think, Yes and No. The Bald One had a sticker for the
girl, from Lark's travels. Lark built a catapult out of a gas tank, with
Iter's help, and launched him. He landed two roofs away, dazed, still
holding the sticker. The girl, watching a squirrel go overhead, began
to wonder whether these people cared about anything alive. Alistrassa
fired her blunderbuss straight up as a warning, after an argument with
herself about which end was which. It was not loaded.

Pudge and Bob took hold of the tree, one each side, and pulled it out of
the ground. The girl held on all the way down and landed on her feet.
Pudge, from the ground, tried to say sorry for suplexing her. "I promise
I'm not scary." Bob took hold of her, gently, with the spirit of Yuhai
in it, and this time whatever was helping her was held off by something
bigger. His sand went all over her. Iter knelt down. "Are you okay? Are
you hurt?" The Bald One landed on the girl's chest and put the sticker
on her forehead and gave a thumbs up, and Mothra landed on the squirrel.
And the girl, who could tell that at least this one meant it, began to
cry. "Please don't hurt me. I'm so sorry." She flinched when Iter
stroked her hair. She curled up and did not try to run.

Papers were blowing away into the night. More were sticking out of her
clothes.

Iter finished sewing the parasol on the way back, and her spores healed
everyone. Abjuration at night was red velvet and runes and tea, five
second-years practising inscription in spirals slightly out of step,
and Penny asleep under a blanket. Snarfa was at his desk with his leg in
a cast. He had broken it clean in half. Worth it, though.

Ada, limping, told them her story. As Studentsson she had enrolled in
every class, got into one in every school but Divination, and talked
the professors into letting her copy their ledgers. The line that set
her off was two bat wings for Transmutation, at four hundred gold bars.
A bat wing runs three gold and sixteen silver to four gold and
forty-eight, by grade. Rare, seasonal. Four hundred bars would keep the
entire Western Garrison, with supplies, for about eight months. The next
day her own notes read four gold, eighteen silver. And the line at the
bottom of the page, where she had spelled out a king's ransom in words,
had changed too. "I saw what I saw. I just can't prove it." Her
supervisor had been vaguely interested. The line vanished the same day.
He had access to her desk, and had napped in his chair all day while
she watched.

Iter went off to a quiet nook with her mushrooms to talk to the Red
Lady. Pudge was scared to stay in the College, having lied in it. The
girl said she knew a safe place. She knew the way in, and the owners,
and they would not report anybody. Her name was Ember.

Over the river, past an inn, in a quieter part of town, stood an old
two-storey schoolhouse with a red tin roof: children's desks, a
chalkboard, beanbags, and no class for some years. Upstairs, rows of
musty shelves: the history of the people who lived here before, the
forest nearby, land taken, the books other people had tried to burn.
Lark pitched her tent in the middle of the floor. It was bigger inside.
Ember told her that her mother had worked in the Sharp Forest a couple
of decades ago, protecting it. They looked at each other awkwardly,
with no animosity and no connection yet.

Then Alistrassa put her lens to the ledgers. It finds what she is
looking for, and what she is looking for is whatever is killing her.
The brightest thing in the room, by a mile, was her own bag of sand. But
the papers glowed too, faintly, in some letters and not others: mostly
numbers, scattered across the schools, in Abjuration, Restoration,
Evocation and Conjuration and not the other two. On some pages it was
the name of the item that had changed, not the price. On Haldane &
Daughters' card, every item name had been changed. And the glow was not the blue
of every magical writing they had ever seen. It was a very light beige,
like a fine white sand. Like the sand in her satchel. Ember, taking
notes under a sheet of paper, called it inflation of the numbers to
hide laundering. "We're on the same page," said Alistrassa.

Pudge turned round three times and went to sleep on the floor. He likes
libraries. Alistrassa sat up in a trance with the papers. Bob washed
at a basin downstairs, brushed his sand into a leather pouch, and found
clothes in the lost-and-found, the trousers to his knee and the shirt
short of his belly. Then he read about the planes. The celestial and
infernal realms lie opposite each other, up and out from the world.
The Feywild lies between them, the space between, belonging to
neither. The world sits in the middle, neutral. And a treatise called
*Karmic Topology* argued that the shape of the planes might shape karma
itself.

It was three in the morning, and the moon, three-quarters and waxing,
was starting down the sky. Ember restocked her powders and her copper
wire and curled up in a corner with her mother's old book, its red
letters faded, her mother's notes all down the margins. If the book had
had the answers, her comrades would still be alive. She gave Lark a
written account of her mother's work in the Sharp Forest, and Lark was
honoured. Lark borrowed *Karmic Topology*. She read it four times. Her
people, she remembered, had once done missionary work in this city,
before the trust broke; she did not know why. On the fourth reading she
had it. Karma is between people, and runs like mycelium. "Karma's bent,
because karma's mushrooms."

Before dawn, everyone rested.
