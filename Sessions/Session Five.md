# Session Five — the party, and the sunbeam

*Played 2026-09-30. Previously: Session Four, the couch and Seamus.*

The days were shorter in October, and the evening came on with a chill
that even Abjuration could feel. Lark was still somewhere on the road.
Pudge went down a flight of cold stone steps to the Groundskeeping
office, along a short hallway lit by candelabras, with a ceiling he
scraped. Small wooden placards hung on hooks down one wall, each with a
serial number, his own among them. Most of the hooks were empty. He had
made this walk hundreds of times.

Mark Thornbush said "Come in," and then, "Why are you always so loud?"
Pudge could not help it, sir. Thornbush had heard nothing from him. Was
it done? Pudge told him a lie, the first he had ever told Mark: that
Finnegan had gone to the tavern in Hamlet, but traffic and festivals had
waylaid everybody, and in the crowd the man had slipped away, and the
envelope with him. Finnegan was very small, sir. Slippery. Thornbush
looked at him for a long time and concluded that Pudge would never tell
a lie. He took off his half-moon spectacles and rubbed his forehead.
This was, he said, precisely the only set of outcomes he had not
prepared for. He took a small wrinkled worm out of his drawer, did
nothing with it, and put it back. Then he gave the order, and gave it
again, and by his own count gave it four times: find the envelope, keep
it until Finnegan has it, and come back and say so. Ideally Finnegan
would open it in the Hamlet tavern, though that was not of the utmost
importance. He did not care whether Pudge found Finnegan, as long as
Finnegan got the envelope. There would be a whole Saturday off in it,
and a ticket to Seaside: Pudge could see the beach. "I don't send you
for jobs I want done cleanly. I send you for jobs I want done." It was
the first time Pudge had failed him in years. He did not want Pudge to
feel bad. He wanted him to succeed. Champ.

Pudge went back up feeling sick, and not hungry, which was new. On the
stairs he counted the placards still on their hooks. There were three:
his own; N0008, a number he had seen a few times and never put a face
to; and N0241, with a red X through it. He would think about it later.
Snarfa met him on the stairs, talking into a rectangular metal device
held to the side of his head ("No, it's gonna be sick"), and asked him
to a party in the quad in an hour or so. Normally, Snarfa said, he was
running away from Pudge naked. He jerked a thumb at the basement. Tight
ass, that one.

Up in Abjuration the others had finished with Finnegan, bought canvas,
and started on runic inscription. Alistrassa gave Penny the canvas and
Iter's parasol, and a cup of herbal tea, and Penny started measuring
angles with string out of a book on runic projection mapping. Pudge met
Seamus, a blonde, flat-nosed Munchkin screaming with no sound coming out,
and was told about the unfortunate magical accident. He decided to be
honest. He told them what Thornbush wanted, and then went through his
own pockets and found the envelope, which he had had the whole time.

He had the sense, on a roll of 19 on a D8, to stop there. The College is
an institution built on knowledge, and in such a place a wizard is only
as powerful as his secrets. They were not going to talk about this in
the open. So Bob climbed onto the couch, did what he had seen Snarfa do
for Alistrassa, waved his arms, wiggled his fingers and said "ooh." For a
moment nothing happened, and everyone stared, and he began to lower his
hands. Then a glowing blue dome came down over the couch. It did not
follow him when he moved. At its edge it felt like a sinus infection;
you could hear yourself through your own bones, as if speaking
underwater. Penny, outside it, heard nothing. There was not even any
residue on his hands. Nobody else claimed it.

Inside the dome they worked it through. Hamlet is not the Don's
territory; it is out past the city, a good way past the squirrel tree.
It stopped being a township some years ago, has no police, and holds
fewer than a hundred people, three of whom have died in the last three
years, all naturally. A death there would be talked about, not
investigated: a spectacle in a public place, with nobody to follow it
up. The rune had not burned itself up on Pudge. And then Alistrassa and
Pudge both remembered something. Every magical writing in the College,
and in their lives, had been in blue ink. The rune was black.

Somebody retold the arm plan for Pudge's benefit. Seamus found it
somewhat problematic. They put it on hold. Iter handed Seamus's rope to
Pudge, who carried him from then on like a handbag.

Sunset went to dusk, and through the dome the party arrived as light and
vibration. The quad was a wall of sound. Students packed in, some
professors among them, naked, and Snarfa was up on a floating stage with
a microphone and a band: the Snarfettes, written in red on the drum —
a goblin, a drow, and a boy of about twelve in black face paint called
Jimmy, playing a guitar with no holes and no cable that came out of the
air enormous. Snarfa had picked him up in a small town. You have to play
for people, man, not Hamlet. Pudge thought Snarfa sang a little too
well, and filed it away. Students tried to carry Pudge. Snarfa winked.

Ra came in on the downbeat: the sky went bright, there was a large crack
and some smoke, and there was the sun god, eagle-headed and golden, a
beam going up from his head miles into the sky. The mosh pit climbed
him. His chest beat in time with the bass.

Then the stage went dark, and a column of white mist, lit from inside,
rose hundreds of feet and fell over into a dome above the whole city.
Calliope, eldest of the Muses, came out of it: long grey hair, eyes that
changed colour at every angle. She winked at Snarfa, took the
microphone and sang, and it took everything you had to look away. Snarfa
came down to say hello. She was an old friend. She was once his muse.
Iter read him as smitten, bashful as ever. It was always about the
music.

Ra's high five landed like a thunderclap and put two people on the
floor. He was the sun god, he explained, and for a moment it was noon.
"What's up, Alistrassa? Why don't you cut some runes?" To Bob he said
"Welcome, brother," and mentioned that he and the Moon went way back.
They used to date. It did not go well. They were still friends. He
should call her. A single tear fell from him onto a small Munchkin. Bob,
who knows perfectly well that the Sun is what lights the Moon, kept it
to himself, let the god comfort him, and decided he hated him. Then he
went to the mosh pit and moshed, violently and confused.

A naked professor took a pull from a flask of iridescent blue liquid
that went red, then green, then orange. His eyes went wide and then
rectangular, with rainbows flitting across them, and he sprinted round
the party. Asked what he had drunk: "I don't know, man. Looks good,
though." His name was Fillibus. Iter led a drow dance, and a large crowd
followed her. She and Alistrassa talked about karma. Alistrassa had a
theory, with diagrams: an elf or a drow comes back as something
Illuminated, a devil or a Fae or a celestial, and a drow was probably a
demon last time. Not her, though; her soul was rotting. Iter, who is
fifty, wondered whether this was her first life. Alistrassa saved the
notebook from a glass of dark wine and said that they should not have
paperwork here.

But she had decided to work. She fetched two armfuls of lenses from her
office, and over the better part of an hour she and the mages built a
stage for a light shell: twenty-three runes on reflectors, amplifiers
and filters, on optically guided tracks. The plan, with Snarfa, was to
get Ra worked up and open it at the climax of Calliope's last song, with
nobody standing in the beam.

"Last one for the night," said Calliope. The crowd groaned. She pointed
at Ra for the closer, and Ra, eyes glowing, the orb over his head
spinning faster, fired before her first note. "That's all I've got,"
said Calliope, and walked off the stage. Pure sunlight poured through the
lenses, cutting the runes into them one after another, and then into the
ground, which began to smell of charred earth. The students cheered.
Alistrassa, moving lenses as fast as she could, saw where the beam was
going: between the Restoration spire, with a fey wellspring under it,
and the Conjuration spire, which has dungeons. "This is not good."

Bob, drunk and still moshing, was in the beam. He rolled a 4 against
its 43, and the top half of him was gone.

Alistrassa, through Iter, asked Snarfa to stop Ra, or at least to ward
the ground. Snarfa could get him to stop, maybe, but Ra was really
strong. He had just killed Bob, and he wasn't even trying. So they
talked him down, a round at a time, Ra's Constitution and the helpers'
Charisma taken off the beam together. "Would you mind cutting it off for
now? You're doing a great job." Forty-three went to thirty-two, and the
air began to smell of brimstone. Somebody tried to wrestle him round, on
a natural 1: the man is a god. More Charisma, and thirty-two went to
thirteen, from an ungodly amount of energy to merely a godly amount, and
purple smoke came bleeding up out of the hole. "Ra. Darling. I think
we're done." Then somebody grabbed Fillibus, rainbow eyes and all, and
Fillibus rolled a 37. "Wait," said Ra, "I thought we were all one. This
is so confusing." The beam stopped. Fillibus's hands sizzled on the
orb. Eleven of the twenty-three runes were cut; the rest would need
another divine beam.

From underground came a blood-curdling screech. A column of bats went
up hundreds of feet. After them, four purple, gaseous, almost human
figures crawled out of the hole. The College took it in its stride: this
is not an unheard-of occurrence. Evocation wizards rained fire on the
demons with something close to pleasure. Abjuration raised domes on the
towers to take the stray fireballs. Transmutation walked up to the hole
and poured sand into it, which set as stone on the way down. The party
kept to the edge. Then, out of the sky, came the inverted head of what
appeared to be a kraken. "Oh, yeah," said Snarfa. "They're doing that
again." Its tentacles took the demons up into the sky, there was
lightning, and it withdrew into its portal. It is important to feed the
deep. Most of the party guests assumed it was part of the show.

Bob, meanwhile, was dead and drunk, and standing again before the robed
figure with many eyes and six pairs of wings, with the Moon in her human
form at its side and Yuhai beside her, confused as ever. "My son, my
favourite acolyte," said Yuhai. "How? Why are you here?" Bob explained
that he was absolutely hammered. The figure held his head steady with
one finger while he moshed in a small circle and took out his heart,
still beating, and set it on the scale, and Bob watched it fall and
fall. The same bloody thing again. The figure wept, tears out of eyes in
every direction, and the Moon stroked the sides of its head. "Darling,
he'll get it one day." Then she came over to Bob. "You're dead and
drunk." He buried himself in her, shifting between heights. "By the way,
that was a wonderful spell you cast, darling. You asked so nicely. You
should do that more." Yuhai said that he must cast spells to make the
world see the majesty and power of the Moon, and the Moon shook her
head. "You have to go now, darling." Bob woke up in the quad, standing,
completely naked, covered in sand, next to his own body, which was still
standing too, and was skeleton from the waist up.

Alistrassa and Iter, heading back toward Abjuration, noticed a student
the other students did not want in the chat: socially awkward, in a
floral Hawaiian shirt, a full moustache and a monocle. It took three of
their tokens between them to notice it. The student's name was
Studentsson, from a long line of students. The shirt was a family
heirloom, all tropical plants she had only ever seen in books. She had
lived in New Squishnesta all her life and was studying magic. The good
kind. She did not drink: too studious. Told that everyone wasted at the
party was a student, she did a double take and wrote it down. Alistrassa
walked a circle of salt round her while they talked, and sent a student
for Pudge. A demon's head, separated from its demon, came bouncing into
the circle and went on bouncing there. Studentsson walked straight out of
it. Alistrassa read her as deceitful, but not malicious. Asked for her
name again, she said Studentsson again, and nobody bought it.

So she peeled off the moustache. Underneath was a perfectly coiled one.
"My name is Ada Crane." She was an inspector for the Castle — an
inspector of the financial department of the City Council. Something
about the books was not adding up. She had been through most of the
Bureaucratic Council; the Academic Council had been very not
forthcoming, more out of disorganisation than malice. Who, she would
like to know, was buying these bat wings at 400 gold bars apiece? She was
the whole team. It was not a well-funded department. The Council
presumed the College was up to something, but its numbers had added up
for years, so she had always been told to turn a blind eye. Now they
went round and round. She would check them one day and the next they
were different, just so subtly. It seemed to be coming mostly from
Transmutation. Somebody offered her lessons in disguise. Somebody else
said she was not the only one asking. They agreed to talk upstairs,
under a dome of privacy, and she would bring her books. "See you in an
hour." She left by the front gate at a fast walk, hips going, elbows
slightly bent.

Snarfa stumbled. Iter caught him, and kissed him, and over by the stage
Calliope saw, and turned away, and only Iter noticed. Snarfa considered
where they might go: the dormitories have beds but interruptions, the
offices are quiet but the professors have keys, and the lecture halls
are empty. Iter and Snarfa left together, and the others saw them go.

Alistrassa sat with Bob beside the rest of him. The demons had been
eaten. Ra was tripping. Snarfa was out of spell slots. Upstairs, in an
hour, there would be an inspector with her books.
